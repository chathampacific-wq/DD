1. Wire size (diameter)

  • 10-coil method: place a tape measure between two coils and measure the length of 10 coils. Divide by 10. Example: 10 coils measure 2.5 inches → wire diameter is 0.250 inches. Using more coils (20) gives a more accurate result.
  • 20-coil ruler: a specialty ruler available from most spring manufacturers — place it between two coils, count 20 coils, and read the wire size directly off the ruler.
  • Spring wire gauge tool: a tool with a series of V-shaped or curved teeth sized to match each wire diameter. Place each gauge on the spring body until you find the one whose teeth fit uniformly into the grooves of all coils.
  • Important: make sure there are no gaps between the coils when using any multi-coil method — gaps will throw off the measurement.

2. Inside diameter (I.D.)

  • Measure straight across the inside of the coil — I.D. is the standard reference dimension, not O.D. Use calipers or a tape measure if you have access to the end of the coils. Measure the largest distance from one inside edge to the other.
  • If the end of the spring isn't accessible, measure the outside diameter and subtract 2× the wire diameter to get the I.D.
  • Check the cone markings first — many cones have pattern marks from the manufacturer indicating the spring size, which can save you the measurement entirely.
  • Important: a spring's diameter decreases when it's wound. All I.D. and O.D. measurements must be taken with all spring turns removed (at rest, no tension applied).

3. Spring length

  • Stretch a tape measure from one end of the wound body to the other — not including the cones. The spring must be straight and without sag, and there must be no gaps between the coils.
  • Torsion spring designs are fairly forgiving on length — a measurement tolerance of ±1/2 inch is a good target.
  • Account for spring growth: a spring grows in length by one coil for each turn applied. It's best to measure with the spring unwound. If it can't be unwound, subtract (wire diameter × number of turns) from the tensioned length to get the free length.
  • If the spring is broken, measure each half separately and add the lengths together.
  • An alternative method: count the total coils and multiply by wire diameter. This is more accurate but rarely used in the field because it's time-consuming and torsion springs don't require that precision.

4. Wind direction — four methods

  • Method 1 — bolt comparison: compare the coils to a bolt thread. A right-hand wound spring screws clockwise into the ground like a right-hand threaded bolt.
  • Method 2 — wire end direction: look at the end of the spring with the end of the coil at the bottom. Wire points right (counter-clockwise) = right-hand wound. Wire points left (clockwise) = left-hand wound.
  • Method 3 — index finger: lay your left index finger over the end coil. If the spring starts at your fingertip and wraps toward your palm, it's left-hand wound. Right hand = right-hand wound.
  • Method 4 — grip: hold the spring with fingers around the body, thumb up. If the last coil ends in the direction your fingers point, that hand is the wind direction.
  • On a standard two-spring system, the springs are opposite-wound — always record which side.
  • Cone color coding: red = right-hand wound (typically left side of door), black = left-hand wound (typically right side). Caution: some cones use universal threads and may be incorrectly colored.

Square wire springs — count coils, not length

  • Square wire spreads laterally as it's coiled, so a square-wire spring's length for a given coil count is not the same as a round-wire spring of the same gauge and coil count — measuring length directly and comparing it to a round-wire chart will give the wrong wire size.
  • To size square wire correctly: count 16 coils, then go back to the nearest 1" or 1/2" and divide by 16.
  • Example:
    Measured: 16 coils = 6" to 6-3/16"
    Goes back to: 6"
    6" ÷ 16 coils = .375" wire

What these measurements are for

  • These four numbers — wire size, I.D., length, and wind direction — are what a spring supplier or manufacturer's chart uses to identify the correct replacement spring for the door's actual weight and travel.
  • This is measurement, not calculation. Matching a measured spring to a chart is different from calculating wind turns from door weight — the chart accounts for the spring's actual engineered rate, which a generic formula can't.

Safety

  • Measure an installed spring with the door in a safe, supported position and the spring's tension accounted for — don't work around a spring you haven't confirmed is properly contained.
  • Measuring is not adjusting. Taking these measurements doesn't require releasing or winding the spring — if you find yourself needing to relieve tension just to measure, stop and follow proper lockout procedure first.

What you're measuring

  • A dock pit holds a pit-style dock leveler. Measure the pit itself — the curb angles and concrete opening — not the existing leveler that's currently sitting in it.

Essential tools

  • Steel tape measure
  • Notepad
  • Flashlight
  • Camera

1. Width

  • Inside curb angle to curb angle.
  • Measure at the front, middle, and rear of the pit — use the smallest of the three readings.

2. Length

  • From the dock face (front curb) all the way back to the rear pit wall.

3. Front depth

  • Top of curb/dock floor straight down to the pit floor, measured at the front.

4. Rear depth

  • Same measurement, taken at the rear of the pit.
  • The front is normally about ½" deeper than the rear, for drainage — don't assume the two depths match.

PROTECT: Technician and Bystander Safety by Equipment

8 equipment categories. Tap a category to open it.

Stored energy, crushing loads, and unexpected movement cause most serious injuries on commercial doors and docks. On every job: lock out and tag out, verify zero energy, control the area, and test all safety devices before the equipment returns to service.

1Sectional overhead doors

Hazards

  • Spring release under torsion or extension tension
  • Cable snap-back, especially at the bottom bracket
  • Door or section falling
  • Finger pinch between sections
  • Fall from ladder or lift

Technician

  • Lock out power. Clamp the door in the track before touching springs or cables.
  • Use correctly sized winding bars only. Never use screwdrivers or improvised tools.
  • Release spring tension before loosening bottom brackets.

Bystanders

  • Keep people and vehicles out of the door path. Cone off both sides.
📷 A wide, heavy sectional door like this often needs more than one torsion spring across the header — each one is still under full winding tension. Tap to zoom.
2Rolling steel and fire-rated doors

Hazards

  • Sudden curtain drop from a released fusible link or governor
  • Counterbalance spring tension in the barrel
  • Heavy curtain or coil falling during bracket work
  • Crush at the bottom bar

Technician

  • Never stand under the curtain. Support it before releasing brackets or tension.
  • A drop test may only be performed by a certified, qualified fire door technician — not general maintenance staff. Clear both sides of the opening first, then reset the door and drop it a second time per the manufacturer's procedure to confirm it releases and closes properly on both attempts.
  • Wear a hard hat and eye protection.
  • On fire-rated doors, check that the hood is intact and fully closed over the curtain and motor assembly, and that the motor itself is undamaged with its cover in place — any of these missing or damaged is a fail point on the annual fire door inspection, not just cosmetic.

NFPA 80, 5.2.3.3: Acceptance testing shall include closing the door by all means of activation — not just a manual drop test. If the door is activated by a local smoke/heat detector, test that detector (e.g. canned smoke). If it's integrated with a smoke alarm and FACP, coordinate with the building owner, confirm supervisory signal programming, and use canned smoke to run every means of activation. If it's fusible-link activated, that requires release at every fusible-link point, not just one.

Bystanders

  • Barricade both sides during drop tests and announce the test before it starts.
📷 Damaged hood exposing the motor and limit-switch assembly — the motor itself is badly damaged and missing its cover. An intact hood and motor cover are both required on a fire-rated door; either one missing or damaged is a fail point on a fire door visual inspection, not a normal installation. Tap to zoom.
3High-speed doors

Hazards

  • Rapid panel movement and impact
  • Unexpected activation from radar, loops, or sensors
  • Stored energy in springs, counterweights, or drive
  • Panel or bottom bar dislodged in a crash

Technician

  • Lock out power and disable every activation device before working in the opening.
  • Test the safety edge and light curtain after any repair.

Bystanders

  • Keep forklifts and pedestrians clear of the opening. Mark the path.
4Dock levelers

Hazards

  • Crush under a raised platform or lip
  • Unexpected platform drop
  • Hydraulic fluid injection injury
  • Fall into the pit or off the dock edge
  • Forklift traffic across the platform

Technician

  • Engage the maintenance strut before going under or into the pit. Lock out power.
  • Relieve hydraulic pressure before opening lines. Never check for leaks with your hands.
  • Secure the trailer or block the dock position.

Bystanders

  • Close off the dock position with cones and signage. No one on the platform during service.
5Vehicle restraints

Hazards

  • Trailer creep or early departure
  • Crush between trailer and dock face
  • Unexpected hook or ICC-bar engagement
  • Signal light miscommunication with the driver
  • Slips on ice, oil, or debris at the approach

Technician

  • Chock the trailer wheels independently of the restraint. Lock out power.
  • Never stand between the trailer and dock or behind a moving trailer.
  • Confirm light signals match restraint status before releasing the trailer.

Bystanders

  • Keep clear of the approach lane and the space behind the trailer.
6Dock seals, shelters, and bumpers

Hazards

  • Fall from an open dock edge
  • Struck by a backing trailer
  • Crush between trailer and bumper
  • Fall from ladder or lift while working at height

Technician

  • Close the door or install edge protection before working near the opening.
  • Secure the trailer or keep the position free of vehicles.
  • Use fall protection and a stable ladder or lift for overhead work.

Bystanders

  • Barricade the position and keep pedestrians out of the backing zone.
7Automatic pedestrian doors

Hazards

  • Crush or pinch at the pivot or hinge side
  • Sudden activation while a technician is in the swing or slide path
  • Entrapment of children, elderly people, or mobility devices
  • Glass breakage or a falling panel

Technician

  • Put the door in hold-open or power it down before working. Use a spotter in busy entrances.
  • Wear cut-resistant gloves and eye protection near glass.
  • Test sensors and safety zones before restoring normal operation.

Bystanders

  • Redirect foot traffic to another entrance and post clear signage.
8Operators, controls, and electrical

Hazards

  • Electric shock or arc flash
  • Unexpected startup during service
  • Stored energy in drives and capacitors
  • Brake release dropping the door
  • Bypassed photo eyes or safety edges
  • Entanglement in chain hoists or drive components

Technician

  • Lock out and tag out. Verify zero energy with a meter before touching conductors.
  • Support the door before releasing a brake or disconnecting a drive.
  • Never jumper a safety device. Test entrapment protection before returning to service.

Bystanders

  • Keep people out of the door path and away from open control panels.

General guidance only. Follow the equipment manufacturer's instructions, your lockout/tagout program, and applicable OSHA, ANSI/DASMA, UL 325, and NFPA 80 requirements.

MAN DOORS: Commercial Steel Personnel Doors

Door construction, frame, hinges, latching hardware, closers, weatherstripping, fire ratings and troubleshooting. Tap a topic to open it.

Fire-rated doors: don't drill, cut, weld or substitute hardware without confirming that the proposed modification is permitted by the assembly's listing. Check the labels on the door hinge edge and in the frame rebate first, and never leave a fire door unable to self-close and latch.

1. Inspection checklist — by component
FrameJamb spread, twist, anchoring, corrosion and strike alignment
HingesScrew size, thread engagement, wear, binding and sag
CloserSweep speed, latch speed, backcheck, leaks and mounting
Latch / lockBackset, handing, latch projection and strike clearance
SealsDoor sweep, perimeter weatherstripping and threshold
Fire labelDoor and frame labels, compatible hardware and self-latching operation

How to check

  • Frame: measure jamb width at the head, middle and floor — they should match (spread). Sight the jambs for twist. Push the frame to feel for loose anchors. Look for rust at the bottom of the jambs and at the strike.
  • Hinges: correct screw size and full thread bite into the frame reinforcement — stripped holes let the door sag. Look for worn knuckles, a door that lifts when pushed up, or binding at the hinge edge.
  • Closer: time the sweep and latch, open hard to test backcheck, look for oil, and check the arm, bracket and through-bolts are tight.
  • Latch / lock: confirm backset and handing match the door, the latch projects fully into the strike, and the door has clearance at the strike side (no rubbing).
  • Seals: sweep touches the threshold evenly, perimeter seal isn't torn or holding the door open, threshold is secure and not a trip hazard.
  • Fire label: labels present and readable on door and frame, all hardware listed for fire doors, door self-closes and latches from fully open.
2. Know the opening — construction & hardware
  • Door construction: commercial hollow metal is usually 1-3/4" thick, 18 or 16 gauge face sheets, with a honeycomb, polystyrene, mineral or steel-stiffened core. Edges are reinforced at hinges, lock and closer.
  • Frame type: welded (set before the wall is built) or knock-down (slipped over finished drywall). Anchors depend on the wall — masonry, stud or existing-opening anchors. Most are double-rabbet with a stop.
  • Hinges: butt hinges (standard or heavy weight, plain or ball bearing), continuous hinges for heavy traffic, or pivots.
  • Latching hardware: cylindrical or mortise locks, exit devices (rim, vertical rod, mortise), and the matching strike.
  • Closers: surface (regular arm, parallel arm, top jamb), concealed in the head or floor, or a power operator.
  • Weatherstripping: perimeter seal at head and jambs, door sweep or automatic door bottom, and a threshold. Fire and smoke doors may need listed gasketing.
  • Fire ratings: common ratings are 20, 45, 60 and 90 minutes and 3 hours. The rating is on the labels — door hinge edge and frame rebate.
3. Door won't latch

Check, in this order

  • Closer too weak or latch speed too slow: the door stops just short. Open the latch-speed valve a little (see Closer adjustment).
  • Strike out of line: look for wear marks on the latch. Mark the strike, file the opening, or shim/move the strike. Don't just bend the lip.
  • Door sagging on its hinges: the latch hits low on the strike. Tighten hinge screws (frame side first), replace worn hinges, use longer screws into the jamb.
  • Weatherstrip or seals too tight: new gasketing can hold the door off the strike.
  • Air pressure: building stack effect or HVAC can hold a door open — note it on the job, the closer may need more power.
  • Latch bolt sticking: lube the latch, check the trim isn't binding the lever spindle.
4. Door slams or closes too fast
  • Close the sweep valve slightly to slow the main swing, then set the latch valve for the last few inches.
  • Turn valves in 1/8 turn steps and cycle the door a few times between adjustments.
  • If adjusting does nothing, or the door slams suddenly, the closer has likely lost fluid — replace it.
  • Check the arm is installed the right way for the mounting (regular arm, top-jamb, parallel arm).
5. Closer leaking oil
  • An oily body or oil on the floor means failed seals. Replace the closer — they aren't normally field-repaired.
  • Match the replacement: size/power, mounting type (regular, parallel arm, top jamb), hold-open or not, and any fire rating requirement.
  • Note the door width and location (exterior, high wind) when choosing power.
6. Door drags, rubs or sags
  • Look at the gap around the door — even on the latch side and top means the frame is square.
  • Rub at the top latch corner: door has sagged. Tighten or replace the top hinge, use long screws into the frame.
  • Worn hinge knuckles: look for metal dust and a door that lifts when pushed up. Heavy or high-use doors need heavy-weight or ball-bearing hinges.
  • Frame out of plumb: check with a level; a racked frame needs more than a hinge fix.
  • Dragging at the bottom: check the threshold and door bottom/sweep.
7. Exit device (panic bar) problems
  • Bar won't retract the latch: check the cylinder/dogging and the rod or cable adjustment on vertical rods.
  • Vertical rod device won't latch top or bottom: adjust rod length so both latches engage; check the bottom strike isn't full of debris.
  • Dogged down by mistake: on a fire-rated door the device must not be dogged — fire exit hardware has no dogging.
  • Outside trim won't open: check the trim cylinder/lever is in the right function and the tailpiece is engaged.
  • Electrified devices: check power supply, the request-to-exit and the access control before replacing hardware.
8. Closer adjustment — the valves
  • Sweep (S): speed from fully open down to about 10–15°.
  • Latch (L): speed for the last 10–15° — sets how firmly it latches.
  • Backcheck (BC): cushions the door when it's pushed open hard so it doesn't hit the wall. Set it, don't remove it.
  • Delayed action (DA), if fitted: slows the first part of closing for carts and wheelchairs.
  • Clockwise closes the valve (slower), counter-clockwise opens it (faster). Never back a valve all the way out — oil will leak.
  • Spring power is a separate adjustment (or a different size closer). More power = harder to open.
9. Accessibility checks (Ontario / CSA B651)

Barrier-free doors in Ontario: Ontario Building Code (Section 3.8) and CSA B651. Check these on any door on a barrier-free path.

  • Closing speed: the closer should take at least 5 seconds to move from 90° open to 12° from the latch — don't set it to slam.
  • Opening force: no more than 38 N (about 8.5 lbf) on exterior doors and 22 N (about 5 lbf) on interior doors. Fire doors: only as much as needed to close and latch reliably — confirm with the authority having jurisdiction.
  • Clear width: at least 850 mm (33-1/2") clear with the door open 90°.
  • Threshold height: 13 mm (1/2") max, bevelled.
  • Hardware: usable with a closed fist — no tight grasping, pinching or wrist twisting (levers, push bars or pulls), mounted 900–1100 mm (35-1/2"–43-1/4") above the floor.
  • Power door operators: required at most barrier-free entrances and public barrier-free washrooms. Push-plate centred 900–1100 mm above the floor and clear of the door swing; check the operator opens fully and holds long enough to get through.
  • The local building official and the authority having jurisdiction have the final say — check before signing off.
10. Fire doors — what you can't do

Must always

  • Self-close and positively latch every time.
  • Keep its label — never paint over or remove door or frame labels.

Don't

  • Prop or wedge open, or use a kick-down holder. Hold-opens must release on fire alarm.
  • Add holes, kick plates or hardware that isn't rated or allowed — field mods can void the label.
  • Install a dogging feature, or non-labeled hinges, closers or latches.
  • Leave gaps outside the allowed clearances (generally 3 mm / 1/8" at the head and edges, 19 mm / 3/4" max at the bottom).

Paperwork

  • The Ontario Fire Code requires closures in fire separations to be kept in working order, and it references NFPA 80, which calls for fire door assemblies to be inspected and tested at least annually with written records. Note deficiencies on the job sheet.
  • Canadian fire door labels are usually ULC, cUL or Warnock Hersey (WH) — record the rating and label number.
11. What to measure for replacement hardware
  • Door: width, height, thickness (1-3/4" is standard commercial), hand (stand on the outside — hinges on the left = left hand).
  • Hinges: size (e.g. 4-1/2" x 4-1/2"), weight (standard / heavy), number per door, and backset.
  • Lock / latch: backset (2-3/4" is common), function (classroom, storeroom, passage…), lever style and finish.
  • Closer: mounting type, arm, power size, hold-open, finish.
  • Exit device: rim / vertical rod / mortise, door width, fire rated or not, trim function.
  • Take photos of the labels and the existing hardware — add them to Field Findings.

Voltage check (power present?)

  • Meter set to VAC, leads across line terminals — confirms supply is live before you touch anything downstream.
  • No reading at the disconnect but breaker is on → check upstream at the panel; open neutral or single-phase loss on 3-phase feeds can look like "no power" at the unit.
  • Full voltage at line side, nothing at load side of a contactor/relay → that component is open. Replace it.

Continuity check (power off, always)

  • Meter set to continuity/ohms — used on safety circuits, limit switches, edges, and fuses. Isolate the component from the circuit first or you'll get a false reading through a parallel path.
  • No continuity where you expect a closed contact → that's your open point. Follow the wire back from there.
  • On a bottom edge or interlock, flex the cable while watching the meter — intermittent continuity under flex means a cracked conductor, not the switch itself.

Three-phase specific checks

  • Motor runs but weak, hot, or backward → check for a lost leg (measure all three phases against each other; one low or missing reading confirms it).
  • Rotation is direction-critical on some operators/levelers — swapping any two of the three line leads reverses rotation.
  • A motor that trips overload only under load (not on start) often points to a lost leg or bad bearing drag, not the overload relay itself.

1. Disconnect & manual check

  • Disconnect the operator from the curtain per the manufacturer's procedure before applying any manual force to the door.
  • Manually operate the curtain through its full travel — open and close. You're looking and feeling for: binding, rubbing, or tight guides; damaged, catching, or separating slats; bottom bar binding; barrel or bearing noise; excessive weight or imbalance; the door becoming dramatically heavier at one specific point; the door accelerating or falling when released; excessive force needed to start movement.
  • Key question: does the door feel mechanically consistent throughout its full travel? Easy most of the way but suddenly hard at one point → investigate the door mechanically before blaming the operator.

2. Reconnect & measure running amperage

  • Reconnect the operator. With the door running normally, use a clamp meter on one motor phase conductor at a time.
  • Three-phase: clamp one conductor (L1, L2, or L3) at a time. Do not clamp all three conductors together — the magnetic fields cancel and you'll read zero.
  • Record current on opening and closing for all three phases:
    Opening: L1 = ___ A   L2 = ___ A   L3 = ___ A
    Closing:   L1 = ___ A   L2 = ___ A   L3 = ___ A
  • Single-phase: clamp the hot conductor only, same principle.

3. Compare results

  • Compare measured current against the motor/operator nameplate FLA (full-load amps) and the operator manufacturer's specs.
  • A large difference between open and close current — with supply voltage and motor confirmed healthy — usually points to a mechanical or load problem, not an electrical one.
  • Example (nameplate FLA 4.2 A):
    Open ≈ 3.1 A / Close ≈ 5.8 A → mechanical resistance on close
    Open ≈ 3.1 A / Close ≈ 3.2 A → load is balanced
  • Do not treat nameplate FLA as a hard pass/fail cutoff without checking the operator manufacturer's specs. Running current also depends on voltage, gearing efficiency, acceleration ramps, and actual load — FLA is the rated maximum, not the expected operating point.

What this test tells you

  • This is a mechanical load check on the operator, not a motor test. A healthy motor pulling high current means the load is wrong — a spring/counterbalance issue, curtain binding, guide problem, or seized bearing — not the motor itself.
  • If the manual check (Step 1) felt consistent and the amperage is balanced, the mechanical system is likely in good shape. High and uneven current with a door that also felt wrong by hand confirms a mechanical issue.
  • Always do Step 1 before running under power — a severely out-of-balance or binding door can damage an operator quickly under load.
OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147

Lockout/Tagout (LOTO)

  • De-energize, lock out, and tag the disconnect for any work beyond a voltage check at the terminals — verify zero energy state before touching conductors.
  • Each technician working on the equipment applies their own lock; a lock is only removed by the person who placed it.
  • Never rely on a control-circuit switch (a stop button, a limit switch) as your lockout point — isolate at the disconnect.
Follow your employer's written LOTO procedure for the specific equipment — this is a summary, not a substitute for it.
NFPA 70E OSHA 1910.333

Electrical safe work practices

  • Default to de-energized work. Energized work (troubleshooting live voltage) is only justified when de-energizing creates a greater hazard or is infeasible, and requires its own risk assessment.
  • An arc-flash risk assessment determines required PPE category for any energized work — this varies by equipment, available fault current, and clearing time, so it's determined per installation, not assumed.
  • Use insulated, properly rated tools and meters (CAT rating matched to the circuit) for any live measurement.
PPE categories and boundary distances are equipment-specific — confirm against the current NFPA 70E tables or a site arc-flash study rather than a fixed rule of thumb.

Stored-energy hazards

  • Capacitors hold a charge after power is removed — discharge through a proper resistive discharge tool before handling, not a screwdriver shorted across terminals.
  • Coiling-door and torsion-spring systems store mechanical energy independent of electrical lockout — treat spring tension as its own hazard requiring separate control.
UL 325 ASTM F2200

Entrapment protection (doors, gates, operators)

  • UL 325 governs entrapment-protection requirements for door, gate, and garage-door operators — the basis for photo-eye and edge-sensor requirements referenced elsewhere in this tool.
  • Photo-eye (Type A) sensors must be mounted no higher than 6 inches above the floor — this is a UL 325 baseline, not a suggestion; a misaligned or improperly mounted eye above that height doesn't meet the standard even if it "works."
  • ASTM F2200 covers automated gate construction and applies where gate operators are involved.
  • Never defeat or bypass an entrapment-protection device and return the equipment to service — a device confirmed faulty takes the equipment out of service until repaired.
ANSI/DASMA 102

Spring and hardware safety (rolling steel / sectional doors)

  • ANSI/DASMA 102 covers commercial and industrial garage door standards, including counterbalance and hardware requirements referenced in the spring-related diagnostics elsewhere in this tool.
  • Use manufacturer-specified winding bars only, sized to the shaft — never a substitute tool.
  • Keep bystanders clear of the winding-bar arc and the door's full range of travel during any spring adjustment.

General field practice

  • When in doubt about a standard's current requirements, verify against the current published edition — standards are periodically revised and this reference is a field summary, not the standard itself.
  • Document any safety device you find defeated, bypassed, or non-functional, even if unrelated to the original service call.

What this is — and isn't

  • This is a framework for working with any operator's fault-code system, not a chart of codes. Blink counts, alphanumeric codes, and their meanings vary by manufacturer and change between board revisions — a code that's reproduced here could be wrong for the exact board in front of you, so it isn't reproduced. What follows is how to find and use the real chart quickly, and how to reason about a code before you even have it.

Find the actual chart first

  • Check the inside of the control box cover or enclosure lid — many manufacturers print or sticker the code chart directly on the housing.
  • Check the install/owner's manual for the exact model — the Manual Library under MAINTAIN is a starting point for each brand's official docs.
  • Newer boards increasingly log faults to a companion smartphone app or an onboard display with more detail than a blink pattern — check for one before assuming LED-only.
  • Once you find the chart for a given board, photograph it — it saves the next tech (or you, next visit) from repeating this step.

How codes are typically communicated

  • LED blink patterns — a count of flashes, sometimes with a distinct pause or a second color to separate the tens digit from the ones digit. Read the full pattern through at least two repeats before acting on it.
  • 7-segment or alphanumeric displays — a code shown directly as digits or letters, usually decoded in the manual's fault-code table.
  • DIP-switch-era boards — older boards may have no fault display at all; diagnosis falls back to the mechanism-level checks in TROUBLESHOOT.
  • App or cloud logs — some current commercial boards keep a fault history remotely, which can show a pattern over time that a single site visit won't catch.

The categories most fault codes fall into

  • Regardless of brand, nearly every fault-code system is organized around the same handful of underlying problems. Knowing the category lets you start working before you've even matched the exact code:
  • Safety circuit open — photo-eye misaligned/blocked, edge sensor cut or disconnected, interlock open.
  • Obstruction / overcurrent — the door met resistance, or the motor is drawing more current than expected.
  • Travel / limit error — the operator lost track of open or closed position, or a limit switch/encoder disagrees with expected travel.
  • Motor or encoder fault — the operator isn't seeing expected feedback from the motor itself.
  • Communication / board fault — an internal board error, often requiring board-level diagnosis rather than a field fix.
  • Low voltage / power fault — incoming or control-circuit voltage outside expected range.

Procedure when you get any fault code

  • Read and record the exact code or blink pattern before touching anything — resetting first destroys the evidence.
  • Use the category above to point yourself toward an obvious real-world cause (a code that looks like "safety circuit" → go look at the photo-eyes and edge first) while you track down the exact chart.
  • Confirm the code's meaning against that specific board's own documentation — don't rely on a code number relayed secondhand or remembered from a different job.
  • Avoid repeatedly power-cycling to clear a code without understanding it first — it erases fault history and can mask a developing problem until it fails in a worse way.
  • Log the code, what it turned out to mean, and what fixed it — for this site and for the next technician who sees the same board.

Caution

  • A fault code is a starting point, not a diagnosis. The same code number can mean different things across board revisions from the same manufacturer, let alone between brands — always verify against the documentation for the exact board in front of you before acting on it.
